Two pilot studies, one problem

Detectors of AI-generated music work well on the examples they were trained on and fail on new ones. I tested this in audio and in symbolic music.

Study 1 · Audio

AI Music Detection on Unseen Generators

Independent pilot study · 2026 · FakeMusicCaps · PyTorch

New AI music generators come out every few months, and a detector is only useful if it still works on the ones it has never seen. I trained detectors on real music and on clips from four text-to-music models, then tested them on a fifth model left out of training, rotating through all five. The detectors read log-mel spectrograms: one is a logistic regression on simple spectral statistics, the other a small CNN. I also tried two ways to stop them from relying on each generator’s overall frequency shape.

0.975balanced accuracy of the CNN on generators seen in training
0.50share of clips from an unseen generator that it flags as AI
0.21AUC on unseen MusicLDM for a spectral-statistics detector: rated more real than real music
  • Data. 2,856 real MusicCaps clips and 27,605 AI clips from five text-to-music models, all 10 s at 16 kHz. Train and test are split by MusicCaps caption, so the same prompt never appears on both sides.
  • Test. Each generator is left out of training in turn, then the detector is scored on it. This is the situation a streaming platform faces when a new model is released.
  • No single fix. Removing each clip's spectral envelope raises AUC on MusicLDM from 0.69 to 0.90 but lowers it on MusicGen and Stable Audio Open.
  • Architecture tracks difficulty. Generators that decode through a mel-spectrogram VAE and HiFi-GAN transfer to each other. MusicGen and Stable Audio Open stay hard (detection 0.18–0.34).

Limits: only 52% of real MusicCaps clips could still be downloaded; real clips are lossy YouTube audio while AI clips are lossless; 3 seeds; five open-source generators.

Study 2 · Symbolic music

Testing a Beethoven Classifier on Recombined Imitations

Reproduction and extension of Galajda & Hua (IEEE MIPR 2024) · 2026 · MAESTRO v3 · PyTorch

Some AI systems imitate a composer by retrieving real bars from that composer’s music and joining them together. I wanted to know whether a composer classifier can tell such an imitation from the real thing. I reproduced a published “is this Beethoven?” classifier (Galajda & Hua, 2024) on the MAESTRO piano dataset, built imitations by splicing real Beethoven bars from different works, and then trained a second detector that looks only at how one bar leads into the next.

0.96AUC of the reproduced GRU classifier, stable under piece- and year-grouped splits
83–92%of spliced segments classified as Beethoven (real Beethoven: 83%)
0.94–0.96AUC of a transition detector separating spliced from real 32-bar segments
  • Reproduction. A two-layer GRU on 27 features per 2-second bar, as in Galajda & Hua (2024). AUC stays at 0.96 when works or recording years are held out.
  • Recombination. Splicing bars from different Beethoven works does not lower the score. Splicing even raises it for other composers' music, from 8% to 25%.
  • Transitions. A detector that sees only the join between two bars (key change, melodic leap, density jump, held notes) separates spliced segments from real ones.
  • The same generalization gap. Trained only on random splices, the detector drops to AUC 0.73 on splices chosen to sound smooth; trained the other way, it drops to 0.56. Smooth splices overshoot: their mean melodic leap is 1.6 semitones, against 9.1 in real Beethoven.
